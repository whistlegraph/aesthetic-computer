Who is applying
A composer who builds his own instruments. For six years the instruments have been software he wrote himself — notepat, a polyphonic touch instrument in any browser; a chromatic sampler; drum machines in a menu bar; and KidLisp, a live-coding language where the score and the synthesizer are the same text. He composes bottom-up on them, performs them live, and releases the results as records.

Princeton is where the ensemble I care about was invented. §Pitch

Most computer musicians compose on instruments someone else made. For six years I have written my own — not plugins inside a commercial DAW, but complete instruments that live at web addresses anyone can open. I compose on them bottom-up the way one writes for acoustic instruments, perform them live, and release the results as records under my own imprints. The instrument, the performance, and the recording are one material, and all of it is made in public.

The homecoming argument.

In 2005 the Princeton Laptop Orchestra proved that a room of networked laptops is a real ensemble — and at $1,500 a seat, the form stayed locked inside wealthy universities. My instruments run in any browser tab, and my operating system boots a fifty-dollar surplus laptop into a complete instrument in seven seconds. That is the laptop orchestra at 1/100th the cost of a seat, able to leave the campus — and this fellowship is the chance to bring the result home to argue with its origin, in the one music community where that lineage is a living practice rather than a citation.

What two years buys.

A body of concert works composed for these instruments, performed live, and engraved as scores a stranger can both read and re-run — whistlegraph notation on one face, the literal source on the other. And a course that is the practice itself: build an instrument, then perform on it, run as an ensemble with a concert at the end and a published repertoire the next class inherits.
