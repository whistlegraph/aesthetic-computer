Project title
Whistlegraph Chamber — a drawing you sing, for four players and a room
Goal and innovation~450 words
A twenty-five-minute work for a four-person ensemble in which the score is drawn in front of the audience. One player draws; three play. Every line is a sung or played phrase, the drawing accumulates on a lit board until the picture is the whole piece, and the players read the board rather than stands. A whistlegraph is at once the notation, the conducting gesture (speed and pressure of the hand are the phrasing) and the learning system (memorizable in one viewing), so the ensemble is open at its edges: the audience reads the same page and plays the last movement. The repertoire is a public index of 1,084 coded pieces at whistlegraph.org; the grant funds the first ensemble to prove the book.
Impacted communities
Los Angeles chamber players who want repertoire without a stand part; general audiences, including children and families, who perform the closing movement themselves; other ensembles and teachers, who receive the open edition. NELA Computer Club is the first place the edition is handed out.
Impact and assessment
Four counted measures: the share of the house that plays the last movement (target: more than half); rehearsals needed before the players can perform a movement without watching the drawer's hand (target: three or fewer); ensembles and classrooms performing from the edition within six months (target: five); plays of the released recording.
Marketing
Each movement is published as a short video the day it is finished to the whistlegraph accounts (1.35 billion combined views); whistlegraph.org indexes the suite by code; Aesthetic Computer's network carries the rehearsal diary; NELA Computer Club runs a preview night; the first performance is asked of CultureHub Los Angeles.
Sustainability
After the second performance the suite is a book — codes, drawings, recordings, rehearsal guide — under an open license. Whistlegraph Chamber then runs as a pickup ensemble: I bring the board, local players bring the rest, each city pays its own performance.

§Pitch

A whistlegraph is a short drawing you sing. Every line you draw is a line of a poem you sing while drawing it, so the finished picture is a score that teaches you how to play it. The form is six years old. It has been performed by a trio in an Oregon cabin, on a twenty-two-minute chalkboard commissioned by Rhizome, and to a combined 1.35 billion views online. It has never been written for an ensemble.

The score is the performance.

In Whistlegraph Chamber the drawing happens live. One player draws on a lit board projected for the room; three players read it as it forms. The speed and pressure of the drawing hand are the phrasing, so the drawer conducts by drawing. No stands, no parts, one page that everyone can see.

The audience is in the ensemble.

Because a whistlegraph is made to be memorized in one viewing, the audience has been learning the notation for twenty minutes by the time the last movement arrives. The last movement is theirs. That is the innovation claim, in Eastman's own terms: an ensemble whose edges are open because its score is a picture.

A book, not a band.

The repertoire is already public: 1,084 whistlegraphs, each addressable by a short code at whistlegraph.org. The grant pays the first cast to prove the book — compose the suite, rehearse the handoffs between drawing and playing, perform it twice, film it, and publish the edition so the next ensemble does not need me in the room.
