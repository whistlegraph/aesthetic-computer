# Spatial antiphony: arranging motion like music

Willaert's Salmi spezzati (1550) grew out of psalmody in which two choirs sing alternate verses. Zarlino (1558) says the choirs stand "somewhat distant" and each must be harmonically complete alone, since a listener near one hears mostly that one. Willaert elides the end of one verse into the next and unites the choirs only at climaxes; Gabrieli's In ecclesiis makes the tutti a short Alleluia refrain between solo sections. Bryant shows the St Mark's choirs often stood close together; the phrasing carried the effect. Alternation is a phrase unit, the tutti is a refrain, and each side must make sense on its own.

Henry Brant calls space the "4th dimension" after pitch, rhythm and timbre: separation disentangles dense, contrasting polyphony and does nothing for unified material.

Stockhausen's Gruppen (Cologne, 24 March 1958) sets three orchestras left, front and right so "a single musical process passes from one orchestra to another." The brass passage (group 119) leaps right, center, left on downbeats while solos stay put. It ends on tremolo strings, a cowbell, one wind note. For Kontakte (1958 to 1960) he built the Rotationstisch, a directional speaker turning inside four microphones; spun fast, rotation stops reading as place and becomes wah-wah amplitude and Doppler modulation. Its moment form is held together by measured pauses.

Xenakis' Terretektorh (Royan, 1966) scatters 88 players among listeners and opens with three minutes of one note passed around. Persephassa (Persepolis, 1969) rings the audience with six percussionists and builds an accelerando to 360 BPM, one rotation of rolled accents around the six every second, cut by silences and stochastic clouds. Acceleration of motion is the form.

Boulez's Répons (1981 to 1984) puts the ensemble in the middle and six soloists around the audience; the soloists are withheld, and their entry is the "response." John Luther Adams' Inuksuit and Sila surround a wandering audience and end by thinning, not converging. Cardiff's Forty Part Motet plays Tallis through 40 speakers in eight groups of five around an oval, one voice per speaker, in a 14 minute loop of which three minutes is intermission chatter, a composed rest.

PLOrk (2005) uses hemispherical speakers so each laptop radiates like an instrument; the 2006 ICMC paper stresses that this lets one "spatially associate a sound source with a specific person," with roles assigned by seat. Cook and Wang's Gamelan Taiko-Fusion Band and Trueman's PLOrk Drones treat each seat as a discrete voice on a shared pulse, never panning.

Féron, Frissen, Boissinot and Guastavino (JASA 2010) found listeners follow a smooth circular path up to about 2.5 to 2.8 rotations per second for noise; for harmonic tones the limit is lower and falls as the fundamental rises. At 112 BPM a beat is 0.536 s and a 4/4 bar 2.14 s. In a 5 to 7 m ring neighboring seats are 2.5 to 3.5 m apart; sound crosses in 7 to 10 ms, so simultaneous onsets from two seats fuse into one phantom image, while onsets offset by more than about 40 ms read as two events.

Recommendations

1. Appear (0:00 to 1:44). Pin everything to seat 1 for 32 bars (to about 1:09). No orbits. At bar 33 seat 4 answers with one 4 bar phrase, then one bar of silence. The surround is withheld, as in Répons.

2. Ring (1:44 to 3:09). Introduce the circle as hops, not glides: one 4 bar phrase from seat 1, the same from seat 2, clockwise, one seat per phrase (8.6 s per hop); six phrases complete one circuit in 24 bars. A low sine drone stays pinned to seat 1 as anchor.

3. Echoes (3:09 to 5:09). Two interleaved choirs: odd seats (1, 3, 5) and even (2, 4, 6). Call 2 bars odd, answer 2 bars even, the answer varied (triangle for sine, a third lower, 6 dB softer). Each seat's part harmonically complete on its own. First half: phrases end before the next begins. Second half: the answer starts on the call's last beat.

4. Echoes refrain. Every 16 bars all six seats sound one chord with identical onset for 2 bars, then 1 bar of full silence. The only tutti before Lift.

5. Echoes decay. Once per 8 bars a note at seat 1 repeats at seats 4, 5, 6, one beat later and 6 dB quieter each time.

6. Climb (5:09 to 7:26). The first orbit, one voice only. Step the clockwise hop rate every 8 bars: one hop per bar (orbit 12.9 s), per half note (6.4 s), per beat (3.2 s), per eighth (1.6 s) in the final 8 bars. Harmony stays pinned; register rises with speed.

7. Converge (7:22 to 7:26). For the last 2 beats of Climb, drop the orbit period below 0.5 s (over 2 rot/s) so it blurs to tremolo. One beat of silence. The D chord lands from all six seats at once. Blur is a cadence, used once.

8. Lift (7:26 to 9:43). Surround, not motion: six voices, one pinned per seat. Two inner voices counter-rotate, one clockwise, one counterclockwise, each one orbit per 16 bars (34 s), crossing at seats 1 and 4 every 8 bars. Noise breath from all six at equal fixed level.

9. Lift climax (about 8:35). The Gruppen leap: a chord passed 1, 3, 5, 1 on downbeats, then 2, 4, 6, then tutti 2 bars. Adjacent hops read as travel, skipped seats as antiphony.

10. Return (9:43 to 12:00). Ring in reverse: the 4 bar phrase counterclockwise 6 to 1, dropping one voice per hop. Rests grow between phrases: 1 beat, 2 beats, 1 bar, 2 bars, the longest after seat 4 so the silence sits behind the listener.

11. Vanish (12:00 to 13:07). Penultimate phrase from seat 4 alone, final tone from seat 1 alone fading over 8 bars: the last motion is an approach from behind to front. Then 2 bars of programmed silence.

12. Rate rules. Hops use hard pins (crossfade under 30 ms); longer crossfades between neighbors make a phantom between seats. Glides use equal-power crossfades only for orbit periods of 3 s or more. Periods of 0.7 to 1.6 s read as spin, followable for noise, marginal for sines. Under 0.5 s reads as modulation. Sines above about 1 kHz lose motion first: orbit low sines and triangles, let noise spin fastest. One moving voice at a time except the Lift pair; every section keeps one 2 bar silence and one single-seat passage.
