## Proposal, section 1 of 3 — How the fellowship advances your work *(250 words max)*

I am a composer who builds his own instruments. For six years I have written
them as software — notepat, a polyphonic touch instrument that runs in any
browser; a chromatic sampler; drum machines that ship in a menu bar; and
KidLisp, a small live-coding language where the score and the synthesizer are
the same text. I compose bottom-up on these instruments the way one writes
for acoustic ones, perform them live, and release the results as records
under my own imprints. The instrument, the performance, and the recording are
one material, and I make all of it in public.

Princeton is where the ensemble I care about was invented. The Princeton
Laptop Orchestra proved in 2005 that a room of networked laptops is a real
instrument, and I have published on its lineage; my contribution is dropping
the cost of a seat by two orders of magnitude, onto surplus laptops and
browser tabs, so the form can leave the wealthy campus and still come home to
argue with its origin.

The fellowship would buy the thing my practice has never had: two years to be
a composer-in-residence for my own instrument family. I would compose and
perform a body of concert works for these instruments, engrave them as
readable scores others can re-perform, and do it inside the one music
community where the laptop orchestra is a living practice rather than a
citation — with the people who built it across the hall.

## Proposal, section 2 of 3 — Working with undergraduates *(250 words max)*

I would teach the course my practice is: **build an instrument, then perform
on it.** In the first semester each student writes a small software
instrument — in KidLisp, in JavaScript, or in whatever stack they already
love — and the class becomes an ensemble that has to learn one another's
instruments, compose for them, and end the term with a concert. The
instrument is the composition exam: if no one else can play it, it is not
finished. In the second semester the ensemble goes further — writing graphic
scores for the instruments, performing on surplus hardware, and publishing
both the instruments and their scores so the next class inherits a repertoire
rather than a syllabus.

I have taught this material for years — digital media and programming-as-art
at UCLA, Parsons, and Southern Oregon University — and I currently run a
free neighborhood computer club where the same lesson lands with nine-year-
olds and retirees alike: an instrument you built yourself changes your
relationship to every instrument. Undergraduates sit at the sweet spot. They
can really program, or really play, and are rarely asked to do both at once.

Outside the classroom I would keep an open studio — an instrument clinic
where students bring half-built synths, broken scores, and pieces that do not
work yet — and I would seek out the existing laptop-orchestra community as a
collaborator, offering my instruments and repertoire to their stands and
learning their performance practice in return.

## Proposal, section 3 of 3 — Diversity & inclusion contribution *(250 words max)*

My contribution to inclusion is economic and material: I remove the price of
admission to computer music. The standard path in runs through a laptop that
costs a thousand dollars, a DAW license, and a studio course to explain them.
My instruments run in any browser tab on any machine, free, with no account;
and my operating system boots a fifty-dollar surplus laptop — the kind school
districts and offices throw away — into a complete musical instrument in
seven seconds. A laptop-orchestra seat that cost fifteen hundred dollars in
2005 now costs less than a textbook. Everything is open source; nothing is
rented.

I practice this, weekly, at the NELA Computer Club I run in a working-class
neighborhood of Los Angeles: surplus machines, all ages, no prerequisites,
music and code taught as one subject. KidLisp exists because of it — a
language small enough that a child's first program can be a song, so the
gate of prior programming literacy stops filtering who gets to compose this
way.

At Princeton I would bring the same practice to the surrounding communities —
Trenton is twenty minutes away — by seeding club chapters with donated
surplus hardware, and on campus I would design my course so it has no
prerequisites in either music or computer science: a student who has never
programmed and a student who has never played are equally qualified, because
building your own instrument is the one assignment where those two arrivals
are the same.
