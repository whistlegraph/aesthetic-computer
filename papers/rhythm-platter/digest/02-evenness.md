# 02 · Evenness, balance, well-formedness

**Shelf:** evenness · **Sources:** `clough-douthett-1991`, `bjorklund-2003-sns`, `toussaint-2005-bridges`, `demaine-2009-distance-geometry`, `gomez-2009-structural`, `milne-2017-balance`, `milne-2016-xronomorph`, `amiot-2016-fourier`

## Maximal evenness

Of all the ways to place `k` onsets on `n` pulses, the **maximally even** set is
the one whose onsets sit as close as possible to the vertices of a regular
`k`-gon inscribed in the cycle (Clough & Douthett 1991, for pitch-class sets; the
rhythmic case is the dual).

**The two-gap property.** A maximally even rhythm uses **at most two distinct
IOIs, and they differ by exactly one pulse** — `floor(n/k)` and `ceil(n/k)`.
E(5,16) has gaps (3,3,3,3,4): four short, one long, never anything more lopsided.

**But the two-gap property is necessary, not sufficient.** It constrains the
*multiset* of gaps and says nothing about their order. At `n=16, k=6` the
multiset is four 3s and two 2s, and both `(3,3,2,3,3,2)` and `(3,3,3,3,2,2)`
satisfy it while being different necklaces — only the first is maximally even.
The correct test is therefore `same_necklace(rhythm, bjorklund(k, n))`: exact,
cheap, and free of tolerance. Use the two-gap property as a fast reject, never as
the decision.

## Bjorklund's algorithm

Grow `k` groups `[1]` and `n−k` groups `[0]`; repeatedly fold the smaller pile
onto the larger; stop when one pile has at most one group; concatenate. The
recursion structure is Euclid's GCD algorithm, which is where the name comes
from. Invented for neutron-spallation timing electronics, not music.

Gómez et al. (2009) give the closed form: E(k,n) is a **main pattern repeated
some number of times, optionally followed by one tail pattern**, with the
repetition counts falling out of Bézout's identity. A generator can use this
instead of iterating, though at musical sizes the iteration is already free.

**Verified.** `build-timelines.mjs` cross-checks our implementation against all
48 published catalogue entries. All agree.

## Evenness as a number: use chords, not arcs

Demaine et al. (2009) prove that Euclidean rhythms are exactly those maximising
the **sum of pairwise Euclidean chord distances** between onsets taken as points
on a circle. That theorem picks the functional:

```
evenness(R) = Σ_{i<j} 2·sin(π·d(i,j)/n)        # chord length, unit circle
```

Not the sum of arc-lengths. `pop/minitek/thesis/dubtek.md` records discovering
this empirically — arc-length "could not separate the claves at all," and
switching to chords recovered the ordering Euclid (0.996) > son (0.993) > rumba
(0.989). The theorem is why. **Chord-sum is the definition; arc-sum is a
different, wrong functional.**

Normalise against the regular `k`-gon's chord sum to get a comparable score in
`[0,1]`.

**Vertex distance `D`** — the sum, over onsets, of the distance to the nearest
vertex of the best-fit ideal `k`-gon (`hypnotek.c:146`) — is an **AC-local
measure**, not Toussaint's. It is useful and should be kept, but it must be
labelled as ours rather than cited to the literature.

## Balance is not evenness

Milne et al. (2017): a rhythm is **perfectly balanced** when the centre of mass
of its onsets, taken as unit vectors on the circle, is at the origin:

```
balance(R) = |Σ_j exp(2πi·onset_j/n)|        # 0 = perfectly balanced
```

Perfect balance and maximal evenness are **different properties**. Every regular
polygon is both, but there are perfectly balanced rhythms that are markedly
uneven (e.g. unions of regular polygons of different sizes). Conflating them is
the standard error.

This distinction matters more here than anywhere else in the platter, because
**balance is the property that maps directly onto physical space**: if each onset
is a sound source on a ring around the listener, the balance vector *is* the
centroid of the sound field. A perfectly balanced necklace is one with no net
direction. See [08](08-spatial-appendix.md).

## Fourier view

Amiot (2016): both properties are coefficients of the DFT of the onset indicator
vector. Balance is `|F_1| = 0`. Maximal evenness maximises `|F_k|`. One transform
yields several measures at once and is the cheapest correct implementation when
more than one is wanted.

## Well-formedness

The word-theoretic face of the same objects: Euclidean rhythms are Christoffel /
Sturmian words — generated by a single interval iterated around the cycle, which
is why they have exactly two gap sizes. XronoMorph (Milne et al. 2016) is a
working instrument built on well-formedness plus balance, with a circular UI; it
is the closest prior art to what `pop/lib` will be and should be read before the
API is fixed.

## tools

- `bjorklund(k, n) -> bits` — validated against all 48 catalogue entries.
- `is_maximally_even(rhythm) -> bool` — via the two-gap test, not a distance.
- `gap_classes(rhythm) -> {short, long, counts}`.
- `evenness_chord_sum(rhythm) -> float`, `evenness_normalized -> [0,1]`.
- `vertex_distance(rhythm) -> float` — label as AC-local, not Toussaint.
- `balance(rhythm) -> {magnitude, angle}` — magnitude 0 = perfectly balanced;
  **the angle is the spatial centroid the `bracelet` lane steers by.**
- `dft(rhythm) -> complex[]` — shared backend for balance and evenness.
- `is_well_formed(rhythm) -> bool`.
