\section{Introduction}

Turf, on the old internet, meant a position in a feed or a rank in a search index. Both of those markets are being repriced to nothing. The feeds are saturated with machine-generated filler that audiences have learned to name and resent~\citep{meltwater2026slop,forbes2026fans}, and the search index increasingly answers questions itself instead of sending the click~\citep{pew2025aioverviews}. Meanwhile a genuinely new kind of visitor has arrived in volume: software agents that browse, call tools, and hand URLs back to the people they work for~\citep{humansec2026agentic,cloudflare2025yir}.

\ac{} is a small, live, URL-addressable creative computer~\citep{scudder2026ac,scudder2026urltradition}. It is not going to out-post the slop, and it should not try. The question this paper takes seriously is where the \emph{defensible} ground is for a platform whose unit of content is a running program rather than a static artifact --- and how holding that ground can pay for itself~\citep{scudder2026sustainability}.

The answer proposed here has two ends, deliberately far apart. At the fast end, \ac{} should become the easiest canvas for the agentic web to paint on: a public, metered MCP surface that turns any AI conversation into an authoring session ending in a live piece. At the slow end, it should grow games for a 1989 handheld in a public garden and sell the harvest as physical cartridges. These sound like opposite gestures. They are the same gesture at two speeds: both make the work \emph{live and claimable} in a way machine-generated static content structurally is not.

First, the flood --- measured honestly, a high-water mark rather than a rising tide. The best available study of the question, Graphite's Common Crawl analysis of 65{,}000 URLs, found AI-written articles crossed 50\% of new publication in November 2024 and then \emph{plateaued} --- 49.9\% in early 2026 --- while 86\% of the articles that actually rank in Google Search remain human-written~\citep{graphite2026plateau,axios2025plateau}. The flood is real; it is not total, and this paper's argument does not need it to be. What matters more is that the audience has named it: ``slop'' won word of the year at Merriam-Webster, the American Dialect Society, and Macquarie~\citep{techcrunch2025woty,ads2026slop}, with negative sentiment majorities in vendor social listening~\citep{meltwater2026slop,fortune2026slopwar}.

Second, the collapse of the old distribution: Pew's real-browsing panel found users click any result on 8\% of Google queries where an AI summary appears, versus 15\% without one --- and click the summary's own source links 1\% of the time~\citep{pew2025aioverviews}; across all US Google searches, only about 37\% now yield a click to the open web at all~\citep{sparktoro2024zeroclick}. Positioning in feeds and indexes is land whose price is falling toward its true value.

Third, the new visitor --- sized honestly. Automated traffic is now the majority of the web: 51\% of all traffic in Imperva's 2025 measurement~\citep{thales2025badbot}. AI agents specifically are a small slice of that --- 4.2\% of HTML requests across 2025 on Cloudflare's network~\citep{cloudflare2025yir} --- but the fastest-growing one, and their signature behavior is asymmetric: Cloudflare's June 2026 crawl-to-refer ratios show Anthropic's crawlers reading roughly 4{,}580 pages for every visit they send back~\citep{cloudflare2026crawlrefer}. The machines read everything and refer almost nothing --- which is exactly why being \emph{callable} beats being \emph{crawlable}. The protocol layer is settling in the governance sense --- MCP moved to a Linux Foundation body co-founded by Anthropic, Block, and OpenAI in December 2025~\citep{anthropic2024mcp,anthropic2025aaif} --- while WebMCP remains an experimental Chrome 149 origin trial, not a standard~\citep{chrome2026webmcp}. Almost no sites are legible to these visitors --- fewer than fifteen expose MCP server cards, by one vendor's count~\citep{discoveredlabs2026webmcp}. Candor about that number: fifteen occupants is equally consistent with land nobody wants yet. The case for early legibility is that it costs almost nothing, not that demand is proven.

Fourth, the counter-market: as the flood rises, \emph{verified human} work becomes a labeled good. The Authors Guild's ``Human Authored'' mark signed more than 1{,}250 authors and nearly 4{,}000 titles in its first year at \$10 a title~\citep{authorsguild2026humanauthored,npr2026cert}; badge systems and verification services multiply~\citep{notbyai2026,edtech2026verify}. Small and partly symbolic --- but the direction is the point, and four dictionaries naming the problem in the same season says the demand side has noticed.

The strategic reading: \textbf{the new turf is (a) being a tool agents call, and (b) making artifacts whose humanity and liveness are verifiable.} Everything proposed below is one of those two claims staked.

Three findings.

\textbf{The prompt is the product.} Across 163 recorded days the prompt averaged 28{,}326 loads per day, peaking at 74{,}343 on August 29, 2026. Whatever \ac{} does next, it happens at a command line that already has an audience --- though an audience of unknown composition: the same scanners that fill rows two, five, and nine of Table~\ref{tab:hits} hit the front door too, so an unattributed fraction of the 4.6 million is machines, and the August 29 peak (2.6$\times$ the mean) has the shape of a crawl burst. A user-agent split of this row is the paper's most important unmade measurement.

\textbf{The dead internet visits us too.} Rows two, five, and nine of Table~\ref{tab:hits} are not people. They are automated scanners requesting Cisco VPN login paths and \texttt{.env} secrets files, absorbed by the catch-all router and dutifully recorded as ``pieces.'' Machine traffic probing for weaknesses outranks all but a handful of our most-loved human pieces. This is the status quo of Section 2 observed from our own porch, and it usefully calibrates the rest of the argument: much of the web's traffic is machines already --- the only question is whether the machines that visit you are burglars or patrons.

\textbf{The long tail is KidLisp.} Sixty-three of the top 100 pieces are stored KidLisp \texttt{\$codes} --- 72{,}961 combined loads of tiny, sourced, forkable programs. The platform has 17{,}538 stored codes and 2{,}969 registered handles. The \texttt{\$code} is already the platform's native, viral unit.

\subsection{The prompt corpus --- newly kept, carefully}

A natural question drove a change while this paper was being written: did \ac{} store what people \emph{type} at the prompt, including the attempts that resolve to nothing? Until September 3, 2026 it did not. Hits were recorded server-side per resolved load only; raw submissions, failed commands, and misspelled wishes were discarded, and the platform's analytics policy (\texttt{shared/posthog-policy.mjs}) still excludes ``source, prompts, media, filenames, handles, or artifact contents'' from third-party capture. The privacy stance was real --- but it meant the prompt was a search engine that threw away its query log, blind to what people \emph{wanted} that does not yet exist, the single most valuable product-direction signal a command-line system has.

So we built the instrument the argument implied. A new endpoint (\texttt{/api/prompt-log}) now records every prompt submission the moment it is entered, and classifies it at ingest: a real piece on disk, a stored KidLisp \texttt{\$code}, inline KidLisp source, an \texttt{@user} piece, or a bare \emph{word} that resolved to nothing --- the last being the miss corpus, the 404s of a command line. The record is anonymous: no user binding, no session binding, and timestamps coarsened to the hour at ingest, so submissions cannot be strung into fine-grained sequences. Ingest is rate-limited per source without storing the source. Email-argument commands are redacted before storage, and the raw text stays inside the platform's own database, never forwarded to third-party analytics, so the policy exclusion above still holds. An admin tool (\texttt{toolchain/prompt-stats.mjs}) searches and tallies it --- top misses, top codes, entries matching a term, totals by kind.

This is a stronger collection than pure aggregation would be, chosen deliberately: searchability requires retaining the submitted text, not merely a daily count, because the product question is \emph{which exact words} people reach for. The privacy discipline moves from \emph{non-collection} to \emph{de-identified retention} --- anonymous, redacted, first-party --- and the corpus of misses becomes an internal instrument (``most-wished-for pieces this month'') that should, over weeks, write the KidLisp feature roadmap and the next-piece backlog from real demand rather than guesswork.

\section{Move One: The Agent-Legible Canvas}

The first claim to stake is the machine-readable layer, while it costs nothing. \ac{} is unusually shaped for it: a KidLisp piece is small enough for a language model to emit whole in a single completion, and the platform already turns stored source into a live URL and a short \texttt{\$code}~\citep{scudder2026kidlisp}. No build step, no scaffold, no asset pipeline. Among creative platforms this property is nearly unique --- and the plumbing exists: the repository already carries an MCP endpoint (\texttt{mcp-remote.mjs}) and a public storage API (\texttt{store-kidlisp.mjs}).

Concretely: publish an MCP server card and \texttt{llms.txt} on \texttt{aesthetic.computer}; expose \texttt{publish\_kidlisp}, \texttt{get\_source}, and \texttt{preview} as public MCP tools; enroll the prompt in the WebMCP origin trial~\citep{discoveredlabs2026webmcp}. Every AI chat session in the world becomes a potential \ac{} authoring session that ends with a human receiving a living, forkable URL instead of another static image.

The keep-earning half is priced at creation, not consumption. Agentic visitors browse enormously but buy almost nothing at checkout~\citep{humansec2026agentic}, so charging agents as shoppers is a dead end; charging their \emph{operators} for making and keeping things is the bet instead. Anonymous rate-limited \texttt{\$codes} stay free --- that is the funnel. API keys with credit packs price the durable goods: named \texttt{@handle/piece} slots, persistence guarantees, HD renders and tapes, airtime on the TV stream.

Candor about that bet: it has no demonstrated demand. Nobody currently sells per-publish credits for generative art, and the 17{,}538 stored codes prove appetite for the \emph{free} tier, not the priced one. So Move One ships as a falsifiable pilot, not a plan: price a publish near the cost of one image-model generation (the nearest per-use comp), and set the kill criterion in advance --- if fewer than one hundred paid keys exist ninety days after the server card ships, the credits layer is wrong, and the free tier stays the whole story while the durable goods fall back to patronage~\citep{scudder2026sustainability}.

\section{Move Two: The Game Boy Garden}

The second claim is staked at the opposite speed, on the counter-market: verifiable, live, finishable, \emph{physical} work~\citep{authorsguild2026humanauthored,npr2026cert}.

\textbf{The proposal.} A side site --- working name \emph{gameboygarden} --- where games for the original Game Boy are \emph{grown} rather than released. Each game is a plot. A seed is the smallest playable thing: one screen, one mechanic, a few kilobytes of compiled C~\citep{gbdk2020}. Growth happens in ticks: a small mutation --- a new room, a new enemy, a tune --- proposed by an agent or a human contributor, committed, compiled to a ROM by CI, and immediately playable in the browser through a WebAssembly emulator. Visitors water plots (votes that steer the next mutation) and take cuttings (forks). Every tick is a commit, a playable diff, and a short reel for the feeds. The garden metaphor is not decoration; it is a publishing cadence and an honest account of latency. KidLisp pieces are one-completion-sized; Game Boy games are grown-over-months-sized. The two moves bracket the platform's range.

\textbf{Why the Game Boy.} The homebrew scene is real, growing, and underserved by exactly this format: at least 51 physical Game Boy releases shipped in 2025, up from 5 in 2018, at 45--70\,EUR per cartridge~\citep{nintendodiff2026gb}; new titles crowdfund routinely~\citep{gamerant2026stains,timeextension2025physical}; and a modern compatible handheld, the ModRetro Chromatic, is in current manufacture~\citep{modretro2026}. The toolchain is mature and scriptable~\citep{gbdk2020,gbstudio2026}, ROMs are 32\,KB--1\,MB, and the aesthetic performs absurdly well in short vertical video --- a distribution machine \ac{} already operates daily.

\textbf{Related work, honestly.} Public, incremental, watchable game development is this scene's \emph{norm}, not a gap: itch.io devlogs are a platform feature, GB Studio projects grow in public repos, and PICO-8's BBS is a living communal cartridge garden whose culture centers individual human authorship under constraint~\citep{lexaloffle2026pico8}. Crowd-steered play has one giant precedent with a sharp lesson: Twitch Plays Pok\'emon drew 1.16 million participants in sixteen days and had to invent its anarchy/democracy governance mid-run --- for any crowd-tended artifact, \emph{governance design is the product}~\citep{engadget2014tpp}, and the garden's one-line ``watering'' mechanic inherits that whole burden. What is actually new here is only the combination --- agent-proposed mutations, vote steering, physical harvest --- and the devlog norm carries an honest corollary: most public projects die publicly. The garden should expect dead plots and treat them as compost, visible in the record.

\textbf{The cartridge harvest.} When a plot ripens, any user can order it as a physical cartridge (Table~\ref{tab:carts}): flashed in-house on writable carts with a USB flasher~\citep{insidegadgets2026shop,adafruit2026flashing}, custom-labeled at small batch sizes, or produced through the scene's threshold-based services once enough orders accumulate~\citep{nintendodiff2026gb,bbbbbr2026vendors}. Each cartridge is hand-numbered and carries the git hash of the tick it was cut from --- the papers platter's print-edition doctrine applied to software: the object is one dated snapshot of a thing that keeps living at its URL. The unit economics should be claimed as what they are: art-edition economics. The 45--70\,EUR band belongs to finished, boxed, published games (Incube8's standard editions run \$59.99~\citep{rpgfan2025incube8}); a numbered cart of a communally grown game is a different product trading on provenance. Writable cart hardware runs \$12--13; a thirty-cart harvest at 55\,EUR grosses about 1{,}650\,EUR against roughly 500--600\,EUR in carts, shells, labels, and shipping --- call it 1{,}000\,EUR a plot \emph{before labor}. That is print-edition margin, not platform revenue, and the paper claims no more: the garden's keep is editions plus the audience it feeds everything else, and the scene's growth curve --- 51 physical releases in 2025 --- says small runs clear~\citep{nintendodiff2026gb}.

\textbf{Why this is the same strategy as Move One.} The garden itself grows an MCP surface --- \texttt{plant\_seed}, \texttt{propose\_mutation}, \texttt{water} --- so agents can tend plots the way they publish \texttt{\$codes}. The fast move and the slow move are one thesis: \emph{be the place where machine effort becomes living, claimable, ultimately physical work} --- the exact opposite of the feed-filler the machines are otherwise used for.

\section{The Option Space}

A strategy paper that evaluates only its own winners is advocacy. Here is what else was on the table, what each costs, and why the two moves above still stand --- with three of the alternatives adopted as complements rather than rejected.

\textbf{Embeddable pieces.} An oEmbed endpoint and one-line iframe for every piece and \texttt{\$code}, so pieces run live inside blogs, newsletters, and docs --- the distribution mechanism that made YouTube, CodePen, and Shadertoy ambient. Cheap (pieces are already URL-addressable), and notably \emph{more} agent-legible than MCP in the short run: an agent that can emit an iframe can place \ac{} anywhere today, no origin trial required. The cautionary comp is Glitch, which shut down app hosting in 2025 citing costs and ``extensive abuse''~\citep{bleeping2025glitch} --- embeds need throttles from day one, and Glitch's orphaned audience is claimable now. \emph{Adopted: this is Move One's missing limb, not an alternative to it.}

\textbf{AT Protocol distribution.} \ac{} already holds an ATProto identity thesis~\citep{scudder2026ac}; a lexicon for \texttt{\$codes} and feeds that render pieces as cards would put tiny live programs in front of the slop-averse early-adopter graph --- the demographic that shares exactly this. Low cost. \emph{Adopted as a complement:} it answers the distribution question this paper otherwise leaves to reels.

\textbf{Live events.} The paper's own thesis says the premium is verifiable human liveness --- and live coding is the strongest liveness proof there is, with a standing scene (TOPLAP, algoraves, ICLC, whose 2027 edition has an open call~\citep{iclc2027}) that \ac{} is already submitting to. Near-zero cash cost, founder time only, and it feeds the channels that actually fund small art platforms: grants, commissions, residencies. \emph{Adopted; already in motion.}

\textbf{Education.} The language is named \emph{Kid}Lisp and this paper had not mentioned children. Scratch's $\sim$135 million registered users~\citep{scratch2026stats} mark the scale of the classroom market, which is also the most durable slop-free human space on the internet, with recurring budgets. Deferred, not rejected: curriculum, classroom mode, and COPPA review are a different company for a season, and the option stays open.

\textbf{Licensing the runtime.} KidLisp as an embeddable SDK other products host --- the tldraw/Lottie shape, and arguably the bigger agent-era claim: a sandboxed art DSL agents can safely evaluate \emph{inside their own hosts}, rather than agents coming to \ac{}. Deferred because it demands API-stability discipline that the instrument's evolving-core ethos would resent; the pilot in Move One is partly a probe of this demand.

\textbf{Arcade cabinets and Discord.} Physical presence without the trademark minefield (Winnitron and Death By Audio Arcade prove the nonprofit cabinet pattern) and synchronous small-group play inside Discord Activities~\citep{discord2024activities}, where \ac{} already has a canonical multiplayer piece. Both real; both deferred for focus. The cabinet deserves one honest sentence: it presses the same leave-the-feed button as the cartridge with zero new toolchain --- the garden wins over it only if the \emph{growing}, not just the object, is the point. This paper bets that it is.

The two build-moves survive the comparison for one shared reason: each creates owned, durable surface --- a callable canvas, a growable object --- where the adopted complements (embeds, ATProto, events) are channels that feed those surfaces. If the pilot in Move One fails its kill criterion and the garden's first plots compost without an audience, the fallback order is written in this section.

\section{Implementation Sketch}

Most of the substrate exists. \textbf{Already built:} KidLisp storage and \texttt{\$code} minting; an MCP endpoint scaffold; the oven build server for remote compilation jobs; daily reel and stream lanes for distribution; the print-edition doctrine with its versioned-object plaque. \textbf{New, small:} the MCP server card, \texttt{llms.txt}, public tool handlers, and credit-metered API keys. \textbf{New, medium:} the garden site itself --- a GBDK-2020 CI lane on the oven, a WASM emulator page per plot, tick scheduling (the existing clockwork lanes are the template), and a cart-order flow backed by one USB flasher and a shelf of writable cartridges. Phasing follows risk: server card and \texttt{llms.txt} this week; public publish tool behind credits next; one garden plot seeded and growing before the site has a name it must defend.

\section{Ethics, Privacy, Limitations}

\textbf{The name.} ``Game Boy'' is Nintendo's registered trademark, and the sharpening precedent is not ROM piracy but \emph{marks in brands}: Nintendo sued accessory maker Genki in May 2025 over Switch~2 marketing~\citep{notebookcheck2025genki}, while compatible hardware that merely describes compatibility --- ModRetro, with its explicit not-affiliated disclaimer and licensed Tetris --- operates openly~\citep{modretro2026faq}. The line the record draws: compatibility \emph{described} is tolerated; the mark inside your \emph{brand} is attacked. \emph{gameboygarden} is the second thing. The site therefore ships as \texttt{gb.garden} (unresolved and apparently unclaimed as of 2026-09-03, though an available domain is not a defensible mark); \emph{gameboygarden} survives only as this paper's working title.

\textbf{Prompt privacy.} The now-live prompt corpus (\S3.1) must not become a keystroke log or a de-anonymization vector, and it retains full submitted text, so the discipline has to be explicit rather than emergent. Three structural commitments hold it: entries carry no user or session identifier and no cross-submission ordering, so a corpus row cannot be tied back to a person or a browsing sequence; email-argument commands are redacted at ingest; and the raw text lives only in the first-party database, never forwarded to third-party analytics, so the policy exclusion on prompt content stays binding. The honest cost is stated plainly: this is de-identified retention, not non-collection, and a sufficiently unusual single submission (a full sentence, a personal name typed as a wish) is more identifying than an aggregate count would be. The mitigation is scope --- the store exists to answer ``what do people ask for that does not exist,'' the analysis works on grouped counts, and any public surfacing shows only entries frequent enough to be nobody's fingerprint.

\textbf{The slop irony.} A garden tended partly by agents risks growing slop with extra steps. Three defenses are built into the form: scarcity (a handful of plots, not a firehose), cadence (ticks are small, legible, and reviewable --- every mutation is a diff a person can read), and the harvest bar (a cartridge run is a curatorial act with money and shelf space behind it, not an autopublish). The garden's claim is not ``no machines''; it is that machine effort here terminates in accountable, versioned, ownable objects rather than feed volume.

\textbf{Measurement honesty.} The hit table counts server-resolved page loads: it undercounts in-session prompt jumps (which load modules over WebSocket without touching the index route) and overcounts anything scanners hammer. The 96.5\% prompt share and the scanner rows in Table~\ref{tab:hits} should be read as strong signals with soft edges, not census figures. The content-share figures in Table~\ref{tab:statusquo} follow the strongest single methodology available (Graphite's Common Crawl panel~\citep{graphite2026plateau}); vendor estimates as high as 90\% circulate but carry no traceable method and are excluded.

\section{Conclusion}

The internet's status quo in late 2026 is a flood of machine-generated static content, a collapse in the old organic distribution, a surge of machine \emph{readers}, and a premium forming on verifiable human liveness. \ac{}'s answer should not be to shout louder into the feeds. It is to hold the two kinds of ground that are actually appreciating: the machine-readable layer, where being one of the first genuinely useful creative MCP surfaces costs almost nothing and compounds daily; and the physical-artifact layer, where a game grown in public --- tick by tick, diff by diff, playable at every stage --- can end as a numbered cartridge in someone's hand. The prompt already has the audience: 28 thousand loads a day, two-thirds of the leaderboard's long tail already made of tiny shared programs. The burglars are already visiting. Time to build for the patrons.
