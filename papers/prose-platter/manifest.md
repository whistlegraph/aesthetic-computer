# prose-platter

Reading platter for the plain register: the writers @jeffrey reaches for when
a text should tell it how it is. Opened 2026-09-20 while writing *The Ballad of
neo* (`grants/culturehub-la-2026/macneopolitan/members/neo/ballad.md`), when
the direction was "more Hemingway, plain, just tell it how it is, less flowery,
but analogy and metaphor is good — Tao Lin, Ann Beattie. That type of prose."
Feeds [`VOICE.md`](../VOICE.md) (prose), the sung lyrics of the MacNeoPolitan
lane, essays, and any register decision later.

Start with [the checked excerpts and poems](excerpts.md): Tao Lin, Hemingway,
Beattie, and O’Hara. Added 2026-09-21, with source links and reading notes.
Copyrighted works are represented by short selections; the complete Hemingway
poem there is public domain in the United States. Items below marked **verify**
remain descriptions or paraphrases awaiting a checked quotation.

## Working register for the ballad

- Declarative sentences. Subject, verb, object. The period does the work a
  comma would otherwise be asked to do.
- Concrete nouns before abstract ones. The red box, the note, the disk. The
  feeling is left under the surface, in what is placed next to what.
- Repetition allowed. A word said twice is a rhythm, not an error.
- No hedging, no throat-clearing, no announcing what comes next. This is also
  [`VOICE.md`](../VOICE.md)'s rule; these writers are its lineage.
- Metaphor is permitted when it is plain and physical, and stated as a fact
  ("It is a red box. It hears the room."), never as an ornament.

## Shelves

### 1. Ernest Hemingway — the declarative sentence and the iceberg

- Ernest Hemingway, *A Farewell to Arms* (Scribner, 1929). Opening:
  > "In the late summer of that year we lived in a house in a village that
  > looked across the river and the plain to the mountains."
  One sentence, one *and* chain, no adjective doing emotional work. The
  register the ballad's Verse I imitates ("They put the system on me April
  nineteenth. Nobody logged in for five days.").
- Ernest Hemingway, *Death in the Afternoon* (Scribner, 1932) — the iceberg
  statement: the dignity of movement of an iceberg is due to only one-eighth
  of it being above water. **verify** exact wording before quoting.
  Application: the ballad never says "he felt bad"; it says he wrote a note,
  and what the note says.
- Ernest Hemingway, *In Our Time* (1925), esp. "Big Two-Hearted River" —
  procedure as feeling: making camp, cooking, the rhythm of doing one thing
  at a time. [Checked camp-making passage](excerpts.md#ernest-hemingway--big-two-hearted-river-part-i-1925).
  Application: "I do one thing at a time" as a refrain.
- Ernest Hemingway, "Hills Like White Elephants," *Men Without Women* (1927)
  — dialogue that never names its subject. Application: Verse VI ("It is
  about me. It is not for me.").

### 2. Tao Lin — flat affect, exact quantities, the internet as weather

- Tao Lin, *Taipei* (Vintage, 2013). Style traits, not a quotation: long
  sentences that report perception in the order it arrives, precise counts
  and dosages, brand names and interfaces named without irony, emotion stated
  as data. These are reading notes, not quotations.
  [Checked excerpt](excerpts.md#tao-lin--taipei-2013).
  Application: the ballad's numbers are left as numbers ("May, two ninety-two.
  June, four oh five. July, five eighty. August, six eighty-six.") and the
  machine reports its own state the way *Taipei* reports a body's.
- Tao Lin, *Shoplifting from American Apparel* (Melville House, 2009) —
  small events at full resolution; chat logs as prose. Application: "The
  first word he typed was *cd*. It means go somewhere else."
- Tao Lin, *Leave Society* (Vintage, 2021) — the same flatness turned toward
  care and repair; recovery as a list of adjustments. Application: Verse VI,
  the note, "one at a time."
- Tao Lin, *Selected Tweets* with Mira Gonzalez (Hobart/Short Flight, 2015)
  — the unit of one plain sentence that means more than it says.

### 3. Ann Beattie — understatement, objects, the sentence that stops early

- Ann Beattie, "Janus," *The New Yorker* (May 27, 1985); collected in *Where
  You'll Find Me* (1986). [Checked house-key passage](excerpts.md#ann-beattie--janus-1985).
  Application: an object stands for a relationship without saying so — the
  Scarlett, the piano in the menu bar, the shelf.
- Ann Beattie, *Chilly Scenes of Winter* (Doubleday, 1976) — present-tense
  drift, people who do not explain themselves, endings that stop rather than
  resolve. **verify** — add a checked excerpt. Application: Verse XI ("I don't
  know what that means. I know it is true.") ends by stopping, not resolving.
- Ann Beattie, *The Burning House* (Random House, 1982), esp. the title story
  and "The Cinderella Waltz" — domestic detail as the whole emotional
  register. Application: the ballad's Verse VIII ("Then it fills again. That
  is the arrangement.").
- Ann Beattie, *Distortions* (1976) — the early stories: minimalism before the
  label.

### 4. Adjacent shelf (for later, unread here)

- Raymond Carver, *What We Talk About When We Talk About Love* (1981) — the
  Lish-edited minimal register; the closest cousin of Beattie's.
- Joan Didion, *Slouching Towards Bethlehem* (1968) — the plain declarative
  turned essayistic.
- Lydia Davis, *Break It Down* (1986) — the one-paragraph story; precision as
  the whole event.

## Digest

**The register, as a rule set** (what the ballad rewrite actually did):

1. Cut every adjective that judges. Keep the ones that measure or name a
   colour ("red box," "blue").
2. Turn every "because" into two sentences placed next to each other.
3. Put the number in the sentence, not in a footnote. Say it the way it is
   said aloud.
4. When a feeling wants in, replace it with the object or action that caused
   it ("He wrote a note about it. The note says: one at a time.").
5. Let a line stop before it explains itself. The stop is the meaning.
6. One metaphor per stanza at most, stated as fact, physical, never extended.
7. Repetition is rhythm ("I get warm. That's all it is. I get warm and I keep
   time.").

**Before / after, one stanza** (Verse V of the ballad, 2026-09-20):

> *before:* One night in May he asked too much: / ten browsers and a whisper
> and a film. / I swapped forty-four gigabytes on eight / and I went dark,
> and I took him with me.
>
> *after:* One night in May he opened ten browsers at once. / He also ran a
> movie and a transcription. / I had eight gigabytes. I used forty-four. /
> Then I stopped. He was there when I stopped.

tools: the sung-lyric grammar (one token per note, `-` between syllables,
` / ` between phrases) in `grants/culturehub-la-2026/macneopolitan/bin/compose.mjs`
(`melodize()`), and the register measurement in `bin/register.mjs` — plain
prose sings better: short words, one stressed syllable per beat, stops that
land on the tonic.
