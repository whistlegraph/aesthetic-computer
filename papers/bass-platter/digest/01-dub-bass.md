# 01 · Dub bass: the weight under the drop, from Waterhouse to 140

Shelf: dub, sub · Sources: `sources.json`

Companion to `02-wub-bass.md` (the mid layer). This entry is the floor: the Jamaican dub bass line and its mix treatment, the one drop, dub techno's filtered chord over a sub, UK dubstep's half-time sine at 140, and where the "mono sine at 40–60 Hz with a harmonic layer" rule actually comes from. Consumer: the sub and bass-layer verbs in `pop/dance/synths/` and the `climbalift` bed in `pop/notespatial/`.

## Unit

Roots reggae one drop sits around 70–80 BPM (the reference article's audio runs at 80 BPM, `wikipedia-one-drop`); at 80 BPM a beat is 750 ms and a bar 3.0 s. Dubstep runs 132–142 BPM, 140 most often (`wikipedia-dubstep`): a bar is 1.714 s, a beat 428.6 ms, and because the drums are half-time the felt pulse is 857 ms, which is the reggae bar's half. Dub techno runs 110–125 BPM (`wikipedia-dub-techno`); at 120 BPM a beat is 500 ms, a dotted eighth 375 ms, a bar 2.0 s.

Register: C1 is 32.7 Hz (midi 24), E1 is 41.2 Hz (midi 28, the bass guitar's open E, `wikipedia-sub-bass`), G1 is 49.0 Hz (midi 31), A1 is 55 Hz (midi 33), C2 is 65.4 Hz (midi 36). Sub-bass is the band under roughly 70 Hz down to 20 Hz, felt more than heard (`wikipedia-sub-bass`). King Tubby's high-pass filter stepped from 70 Hz to 7.5 kHz in ten positions at 18 dB/oct (`audiothing-dub-filter`). A dub echo of about 0.7 s heard four or five times (`terrano-2007-sos-dub-mixing`) is 0.93 of a beat at 80 BPM: off the grid by 50 ms per repeat, which is why it "revolves around the beat" and drifts rather than locking.

## Facts

**The version and the desk.** Dub begins with an accident: in 1968 Ruddy Redwood cut a dubplate of the Paragons' "On the Beach" at Treasure Isle and engineer Byron Smith left the vocal off; Redwood played the instrumental against the vocal at his dance and the crowd sang the missing part (`wikipedia-dub-music`). In 1972 King Tubby bought a mid-1960s 12-into-4 MCI console from Dynamic Sounds; it had a reverb send on every channel and three-band EQ, so reverb could be placed on one element without wetting the rest, and its custom high-pass filter, fitted to strip low end from mic channels, became a swept trademark effect (`degiorgio-2018-roots-of-dub`). That filter was an Altec 9069B, a passive inductor T-network, 70 Hz to 7.5 kHz in ten steps, later nicknamed the Big Knob (`audiothing-dub-filter`). The clang on Tubby's dubs was a spring reverb struck with a stick, per Nick Manasseh; tape delays (Roland RE-201, Echoplex) supplied feedback echo; hard compression on masters brought the snare forward (`degiorgio-2018-roots-of-dub`). The Space Echo (1974) has one record head and three playback heads on a free-running tape loop, with spring reverb built in; Perry used it through the 1970s (`wikipedia-re-201`). Perry's Black Ark (1973–1979) ran a four-track Soundcraft desk and TEAC recorder with Echoplex and Space Echo, bouncing overdubs to compete with 16-track rooms; Tubby designed its circuitry (`wikipedia-black-ark`). Scientist (b. 1960) began mixing at Tubby's four-track desk in the mid-1970s, moved to 16 tracks at Channel One at the decade's end, and worked with the Roots Radics (`wikipedia-scientist`). Tubby treated the console as an instrument, interchanging vocal and instrumental rather than merely removing the voice; the removal of vocals and other instruments is what puts the bass guitar forward (`wikipedia-dub-music`). Veal's study frames the whole practice as studio processing turned into composition (`veal-2007-dub`).

**The bass line itself.** "King Tubby Meets Rockers Uptown" (1974; Augustus Pablo producing and playing melodica, Tubby mixing, Carlton Barrett on drums) versions Jacob Miller's "Baby I Love You So"; Veal hears it as "an insistent, eighth-note bass pattern anchoring a I minor–IV minor chord sequence" (`wikipedia-rockers-uptown`). Player and forum consensus on the recorded tone: flatwound or dead strings, tone control rolled off, playing near the neck, often a foam mute, recorded direct with a preamp or tube compressor to keep it round; Aston Barrett on a P-Bass and Robbie Shakespeare on Fenders, both with early Höfner violin-bass tracks (`gearspace-reggae-bass-sound`). Reggae bass "sits high in the mix" and "often carries a militant feel" (`terrano-2007-sos-dub-mixing`). The one drop places kick and snare (cross-stick) together on beat 3 and leaves beat 1 empty; dropping the bass out on the "one" accentuates the drum's dropped downbeat; rockers adds kick on every quarter, steppers on every eighth (`wikipedia-one-drop`).

**Drop-out and return.** Dub's core gesture is muting: everything except drums and bass, or the reverse, for a phrase, then back (`wikipedia-dub-music`). A common form is two bars out, two bars in, or swapping the two groups at the bar line (`zzounds-2018-dub-production`). Dub "wants to be mixed in real time and preferably on an analogue board": set the delay to about 0.7 s, raise feedback until each repeat is heard four or five times, return the delay to a desk channel with its own send and feed it back into itself gradually; on digital desks, filter the highs off the return and put a limiter on it so the buildup does not distort (`terrano-2007-sos-dub-mixing`).

**Dub techno.** Moritz von Oswald and Mark Ernestus formed Basic Channel in Berlin in 1993, released nine 12"s in 1993–94 under Cyrus, Quadrant, Maurizio and others, then Chain Reaction, Rhythm & Sound, Burial Mix, Main Street and the Dubplates & Mastering cutting room; the style is minimal with delay drawn from Jamaican dub (`wikipedia-basic-channel`). The genre runs 110–125 BPM, 4/4, deep repetitive bass lines, heavy delay and reverb on percussion and melodic parts, sparse chords, the desk as instrument; Deepchord, Vladislav Delay and Monolake carry it on (`wikipedia-dub-techno`). Basic Channel have given essentially no interviews; the prevailing guess is a Prophet-5 (`kvr-2011-dub-techno-howto`). A recipe in the Basic Channel manner: chord stab through a band-pass at about 450 Hz, 12 dB slope, LFO on the cutoff at a low amount, bit reduction to 6 bits, a dotted-eighth echo plus a filter delay for secondary rhythmic echoes, reverb with short decay; bass with a short release, EQ cutting below about 37 Hz, sidechained to the kick with the fastest attack; that tutorial's session runs at 145 BPM, well above the genre's usual band (`esen-2021-attack-basic-channel`). A second recipe: chord through a high-pass, not a low-pass, for the "thin and airy" body; two delay taps, tap 1 a dotted eighth with about 20% feedback, tap 2 at 264 ms with about 85%; send at 100%; EQ the send with a high-pass at 100 Hz, a low-pass at 4.5 kHz and about −7 dB at 600 Hz; plate reverb at a low wet level; a phaser (`douglas-2021-attack-dub-chords`). Rod Modell (Deepchord) says the synth barely matters because "so little of the original signal remains" after the processor chain, favours processors over synths, uses field recordings made while something was happening, and works only at night (`durston-2011-inverted-deepchord`, `russell-2014-vice-deepchord`). Practitioners pair a spring reverb model with a tape echo model (GS-201 type) and tape saturation for the character (`kvr-2011-dub-techno-howto`).

**Dubstep's dub.** The name appears in 2001; Big Apple and Tempa used it by 2002 for remixes distinct from 2-step and grime; roots in Jamaican sound-system culture, UK garage, 2-step and dub; FWD>> (2001, Velvet Rooms then Plastic People) and DMZ in Brixton were the rooms; tempo 132–142 with 140 typical, syncopated, a clap or snare "every third beat"; minor keys and Phrygian modes, tritones; wobble bass "typically produced by using a low-frequency oscillator to manipulate certain parameters of a synthesiser" (`wikipedia-dubstep`). Mala came to dub through jungle, "London dub in a sense", and to Augustus Pablo around 1997–98; he builds bass lines from "a straight sine wave" with EQ and filtering, low-cuts around 40 Hz so the sub is not "flabby", says the FWD>> rig at Plastic People exposed his productions, dates DMZ to 2004 with a first night in March 2005, and gives dubstep as settling at 140 after starting "maybe 138"; half-step is how a fixed tempo is made to feel slower (`mala-2008-rbma`). Skream, 2006: "You can make a bass from anything ... They're just tones"; it is "the frequencies you're cutting and the frequencies you're pushing"; he cuts at 30 Hz rather than 40 because good systems reach 20 Hz; heaviness is "the bass", not the tempo (`skream-2006-rbma`). Loefah's habit is the delayed drop: the bass arrives when he decides it is time, and the empty space around the hits is part of the design (`mesinai-2026-loefah`). A 2010 tutorial: sines are the starting point for a sub, the low octaves put the fundamental around 30–60 Hz (the article says C0–C1; see Debts), do not stack an 808 kick under a sustained sub, sidechain the sub to the kick or to the hi-hat for a shorter duck, mono mode with legato/portamento, EQ by cutting not boosting (`jenkins-2010-sos-dubstep-basics`). A second: two oscillators at −24 and −12, played C2 to C3 so the pair covers C0–C2 equivalents, FM from oscillator 2 for edge, 570 ms release, LFO 2 at a synced 1/8 on the filter cutoff for the wobble layer (`smith-2010-sos-dubstep-secrets`). Forum guidance of the same era: the sub is "a sine wave at around the 40–50 Hz range", and if it must wobble the LFO goes on amplitude, because a low-pass has nothing to remove from a sine (`rendr-2008-dubstepforum-wobble`).

**Where the mono-sine rule comes from.** Vinyl: cutting engineers sum the low end to mono because out-of-phase bass makes vertical groove excursion that limits level and tracking; the elliptical equaliser's crossover runs 75–300 Hz with 150 Hz the common setting (`masterdisk-vinyl-mono-bass`). PA and club systems feed one signal to every subwoofer, so anything stereo below about 100–200 Hz is thrown away or cancels; mono it at the source, using linear-phase or first-order filters, not steep minimum-phase ones (`stewart-2018-flotown-center-sub`). Mix practice: keep bass patches phase-coherent below 100 Hz because "many PAs sum low frequencies to mono"; when layering a sub, high-pass the other bass layers around 100 Hz; sines and triangles with a simple on/off envelope make the cleanest synthetic sub; duck the bass 2–3 dB on each kick to save headroom; if the bass has to own sub-40 Hz, the kick lives in 100–200 Hz, and vice versa; for small speakers a 1 kHz boost or a parallel distortion layer low-passed at 2–3 kHz, checked for phase against the dry bass (`senior-2012-sos-mixing-bass`). The reason a harmonic layer works is the missing fundamental: a pitch is heard from a harmonic set even when the fundamental is absent; telephones pass little below 300 Hz yet a 150 Hz male voice keeps its pitch; MaxxBass (1999) synthesises harmonics above the speaker's cutoff on purpose (`wikipedia-missing-fundamental`). Phones roll off below about 200 Hz and pass nothing under 100 Hz; the band that carries a bass note on a phone is 250–700 Hz; high-pass reverb and delay returns at 200–400 Hz (`lavoie-2026-landr-phone-bass`).

## Rules

R1 sub layer: one sine (triangle if it must be heard on a laptop alone), C1–C2 (32.7–65.4 Hz, midi 24–36), monophonic, legato with a short glide (`senior-2012-sos-mixing-bass`, `jenkins-2010-sos-dubstep-basics`). High-pass at 30 Hz for a sound-system master, 40 Hz otherwise (`skream-2006-rbma`, `mala-2008-rbma`). Mono. No stereo effect touches it.

R2 bass-guitar layer: same notes an octave up when the sub is C1, or in unison when the sub is C2; two or three low harmonics only, low-passed. No source gives the cutoff; a 12 dB/oct low-pass between 300 Hz and 1 kHz, sweepable down toward 300 Hz at transitions, is a rule of thumb, modelled on rolled-off flatwound tone (`gearspace-reggae-bass-sound`). High-pass this layer at 100 Hz when the sub is present (`senior-2012-sos-mixing-bass`). Note length: eighth-note pattern, each note damped before the next (`wikipedia-rockers-uptown`).

R3 one drop: kick and cross-stick on beat 3, beat 1 empty; the bass rests on the "one" at least every other bar so the drum's dropped downbeat is heard (`wikipedia-one-drop`). At 140 half-time the same pattern runs across a bar: kick on 1, snare on 3, bass entering after the kick (`wikipedia-dubstep`).

R4 drop-out and return: mute in groups (drums+bass vs everything else) for 2 bars, swap at the bar line, return on a downbeat; delay tails carry across the hole (`zzounds-2018-dub-production`, `terrano-2007-sos-dub-mixing`). The first bass entry of a piece is late: at least one 8-bar phrase of drums and space before it (`mesinai-2026-loefah`).

R5 sends, not inserts: one spring-type reverb and one tape-type delay as returns; delay 0.7 s at 80 BPM (scale with tempo: about 0.93 of a beat, so 400 ms at 140, 470 ms at 120) with feedback for 4–5 audible repeats; the return is high-passed at 100 Hz, low-passed at 4.5 kHz and limited (`terrano-2007-sos-dub-mixing`, `douglas-2021-attack-dub-chords`). The sub never goes to a send.

R6 the throw: a stepped high-pass on the return or on a stab, 70 Hz to 7.5 kHz in about 10 steps at 18 dB/oct, swept over one or two bars (`audiothing-dub-filter`). A spring "clang" is an impulse into the reverb, not a sample.

R7 dub-techno chord: minor triad or minor 7th, band-passed at about 450 Hz (12 dB) or high-passed and thin, a small LFO on the cutoff, 6-bit reduction, dotted-eighth echo at ~20% feedback plus a second tap at 264 ms at ~85%, plate reverb low wet, EQ −7 dB at 600 Hz on the send (`esen-2021-attack-basic-channel`, `douglas-2021-attack-dub-chords`). Place the chord off the kick; the pump comes from the delay pattern and the bass's short release, not from a compressor, unless a fastest-attack sidechain is wanted (`esen-2021-attack-basic-channel`).

R8 kick and sub: duck the sub 2–3 dB per kick with the fastest attack (`senior-2012-sos-mixing-bass`), or sidechain from the hat for a shorter duck (`jenkins-2010-sos-dubstep-basics`); cut the bass below 37 Hz when the kick owns the sub (`esen-2021-attack-basic-channel`); the kick's body then lives at 100–200 Hz (`senior-2012-sos-mixing-bass`).

R9 mono/stereo split: everything below 100 Hz mono, coherent, first-order or linear-phase filtered; stereo width only on returns and on the chord above 100 Hz; vinyl masters mono to 150 Hz (`stewart-2018-flotown-center-sub`, `masterdisk-vinyl-mono-bass`).

R10 small-speaker layer: a parallel harmonic copy of the sub high-passed at 150 Hz, carrying 250–700 Hz, low-passed at 2–3 kHz, phase-checked; a 1 kHz presence lift if the note still vanishes on a phone (`lavoie-2026-landr-phone-bass`, `senior-2012-sos-mixing-bass`, `wikipedia-missing-fundamental`).

R11 levels: no source gives a sub-to-bass ratio in dB. Rule of thumb: the sub peaks 3–6 dB under the bass layer at the meter, and the harmonic layer 12–18 dB under the sub; if the low end dominates the bus, the fault is the kick sharing the sub band (`senior-2012-sos-mixing-bass`).

R12 tempo bands: one drop 70–80 BPM, dub techno 110–125, dubstep 138–142 with half-time drums; convert every delay and LFO to beats before rendering (`wikipedia-one-drop`, `wikipedia-dub-techno`, `wikipedia-dubstep`, `mala-2008-rbma`).

tools: `subSine(midi, hpfHz, glideMs)`, `bassLayer(midi, lpfHz, decayMs, hpfHz)`, `oneDrop(bpm, halfTime)`, `dropOut(bars, group)`, `springSend(level, clangAt)`, `tapeDelay(beats, repeats, hpfHz, lpfHz)`, `hpfThrow(fromHz, toHz, steps, bars)`, `dubChord(notes, bpfHz, bits, taps)`, `duck(kickTimes, dB, attackMs, releaseMs)`, `monoBelow(hz)`, `harmonicLayer(hpfHz, lpfHz, drive, dB)`.

## Debts

Basic Channel have not spoken; every dub-techno number is a tutorial or a forum reconstruction, and the Attack recipe runs at 145 BPM against the genre's 110–125. Jenkins writes "C0–C1 ... approximately 30–60Hz"; C0 is 16.4 Hz and C1 32.7 Hz, so the octave he means is C1–C2, and R1 uses that. The bass-guitar tone facts (flatwound, rolled-off tone, DI) are player-forum consensus, not a Barrett or Shakespeare interview. The two-bar drop-out figure comes from a search excerpt of the zZounds article, which returned 403 at fetch time. The vinyl 150 Hz crossover is from a mastering-house page seen in excerpt. No source gives the electric-bass low-pass cutoff or the sub-to-bass level ratio; R2 and R11 say so. Loefah's 2004 half-step dating (Horror Show, 28g) is widely repeated but was not confirmed in a fetched primary source and is left out of the Facts. The delay-time scaling in R5 is arithmetic on Terrano's 0.7 s, not his instruction.
