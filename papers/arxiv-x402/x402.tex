\section{Introduction}

A recurring question in this practice is where cultural value, once
accumulated, can actually be captured. A companion RFC answers it for human
collectors by making each whistlegraph a purchasable code that shares in the
music derived from it~\citep{scudder2026funding}. This report asks the same
question of a different buyer. Language-model agents crawl archives
constantly, and a payment protocol now exists that would let them pay for what
they take. If an art archive can sell itself to machines, the whistlegraph
corpus---large, structured, already machine-legible---is a reasonable place to
test it.

The test was built and shipped. This report is the audit, and it is largely a
negative result. That framing is deliberate: the integration compiled,
deployed, returned correct-looking HTTP, and advertised itself competently for
months while being incapable of earning a single dollar for reasons that no
amount of further polish would have fixed. The interesting content is in
\emph{why} the failure was invisible, because each defect was individually
plausible and collectively fatal.

Section~\ref{sec:system} describes what was built. Section~\ref{sec:impl}
covers the repairs. Section~\ref{sec:evidence} reports six measurements,
each independently checkable. Section~\ref{sec:market} places the result in
the wider x402 economy and examines which turnstiles in that economy actually
earn. Section~\ref{sec:strategy} evaluates what to do next.

\section{Context}

\subsection{x402}

HTTP reserved status code \code{402 Payment Required} for future
use~\citep{rfc9110}; for thirty years the future did not
arrive. x402~\citep{x402spec} claims it. A client requests a resource, receives
a \code{402} carrying machine-readable terms---amount, asset contract,
receiving address, network, timeout---signs a stablecoin transfer
authorization~\citep{eip3009}, and repeats the request with an
\code{X-PAYMENT} header. A \emph{facilitator} verifies and settles that
authorization on chain.

Two properties make it attractive for an archive. There is no account,
API key, or signup, so a buyer that is not a person needs no onboarding. And
asking is free: an unpaid request returns the terms rather than an error, so
the paywall is self-documenting to anything that speaks HTTP.

\subsection{The corpus}

\wg{}~\citep{scudder2026whistlegraph} is a graphic-score form where every drawn
line is a sung line. The index behind \code{whistlegraph.org} holds 291 works,
294 candidates, 793 legacy codes, and 2{,}483 published appearances carrying
1{,}526{,}032{,}122 views. It is generated, versioned, and published in three
machine-readable forms: \code{llms.txt} as a map, \code{index.md} as the whole
archive in Markdown, and \code{graphs.json} plus \code{posts.json} as
structured data. This matters for what follows: \emph{the free tier was
designed to be generous, and that generosity was never reconciled against what
the paid tier was later asked to sell.}

\section{System}
\label{sec:system}

Three resources were priced, routed at \code{/api/wg/*} through a single
serverless handler, with a fourth added during this audit
(Table~\ref{tab:endpoints}).

\begin{table}[t]
\centering
\footnotesize
\begin{tabularx}{\columnwidth}{@{}lrX@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Endpoint} & \textbf{USDC} & \textbf{Sells} \\
\midrule
\code{/bulk} & 5.00 & Whole dataset, one document \\
\code{/sources/<code>} & 0.10 & Source videos behind one work \\
\code{/license/<code>} & 1.00 & Signed redistribution license \\
\code{/verify} & free & Checks a license receipt \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}
\caption{The priced surface. Prices are quoted in atomic units of Circle USDC

\label{tab:endpoints}

The license endpoint is the design's most careful piece. It does not pretend
to gate the artwork: the score images and videos are already public on the
CDN, and the receipt says so in its own payload. What it sells is a signed,
time-bounded grant of reuse rights---an HMAC-SHA256~\citep{rfc2104} over the
whole receipt, naming the work, the author, the payer, and an expiry.

\section{Implementation and Repairs}
\label{sec:impl}

\subsection{Settlement}

The original configuration pointed at the protocol's reference facilitator
while quoting prices in mainnet USDC. Those are incompatible, as
\S\ref{sec:evidence} shows by measurement. Settlement was moved to an
account-holding facilitator (Coinbase CDP), which required replacing a static
bearer token with per-request authentication: an EdDSA~\citep{rfc8032} JSON
Web Token signed with the account's Ed25519 key, bound by a \code{uris} claim
to the exact method and URI being called, expiring in 120 seconds. The
credential arrives as 64 bytes---a 32-byte seed followed by its public
half---which must be wrapped as PKCS8 before a standard library will load it.

\subsection{A preflight that distinguishes its failures}

The handler now asks the facilitator what it can settle before quoting any
price, and---this is the part worth generalizing---keeps the two negative
answers apart. A facilitator that answers \emph{no} yields one \code{503}; a
facilitator that does not answer at all yields another. Collapsing them was a
real bug during this work: an early version logged the lookup failure and
quoted anyway, which made a successful \code{402} and a silently broken one
indistinguishable from outside. For a public endpoint the HTTP response is
frequently the only diagnostic channel available, so distinct failures must
not share a status code.

\subsection{Verification that carries its own evidence}

Every license advertised a verification link of the form
\code{/verify?code=\&sig=}. The endpoint did not exist. It also could not have
worked, because the signature covers the entire receipt---licensee and
issue timestamp included---so a work code and a signature are jointly
insufficient to recompute the digest. No amount of implementation would have
closed that gap; the specification was wrong, not the code.

The repair keeps verification stateless, which the design requires, by making
the receipt travel inside its own link as a base64url token checked against
the exact bytes that were signed. Nothing is stored, so the archive keeps no
record of who bought what. Edited receipts fail; genuine receipts past their
term report \code{expired} rather than quietly passing. Checking a license is
free, on the reasoning that charging to confirm a license already bought would
make the signature worth less than the paper it is printed on.

\section{Evidence}
\label{sec:evidence}

Six measurements, taken 2026-08-27. Each is reproducible by a reader with
network access; the first two require no credentials at all.

\subsection{M1: the facilitator settled testnets only}

\code{GET https://x402.org/facilitator/supported} returns the networks the
reference facilitator will settle. Base mainnet is absent
(Table~\ref{tab:facilitators}). The endpoint was quoting \$5.00 in real Circle
USDC on a chain its own facilitator could not touch. The failure mode is
worse than an outage: the buyer signs a payment authorization first and is
refused after.

\label{tab:facilitators}
\end{table}

\subsection{M2: the advertised verification was unsatisfiable}

Shown by construction in \S\ref{sec:impl}: the signed message includes the
licensee address and issue timestamp; the verification URL carries neither.

\subsection{M3: two of three products are free}

\code{posts.json} is public, returns HTTP 200, and is linked from
\code{llms.txt}. It contains all 2{,}483 posts, of which 1{,}332 carry the
\code{contributes} relationship edges spanning 290 of the 291 works. Those
edges are precisely what the \$0.10 \code{/sources} endpoint computes. The
\$5.00 \code{/bulk} endpoint is that same file plus \code{graphs.json}
(401\,KB) with media URLs resolved. Both inputs are free, both are 1.8\,MB
combined, and the machine index tells any agent where they are.

The paid tier therefore sold, at \$5.00, a convenience over two files it was
simultaneously giving away and advertising. Only the license sells something
unobtainable elsewhere, because a signature cannot be forged from public data.

\subsection{M4: nobody ever asked}

Across the retained Caddy access logs, requests to \code{/api/wg/*} number
zero. Requests to the free machine index---\code{llms.txt},
\code{index.md}---also number zero. \emph{Caveat:} these logs rotate by size
every few minutes under production traffic, so the window is hours, not the
lifetime of the deployment. The measurement establishes that no agent asked
during a busy contemporary window; it does not establish that none ever did.

\subsection{M5: no payment has ever settled}

The receiving address holds no USDC. Its entire ERC-20 history is four
unsolicited airdrops---a counterfeit token whose ticker spells ``USDC'' with
Cyrillic characters, a phishing SHIB variant, and two others---none of them a
payment. This is consistent with M1 and M4 and independently confirms them.

\subsection{M6: the repaired rail works}

Queried from the production host, whose address the credential is pinned to,
the CDP facilitator authenticates the signed JWT and reports Base mainnet
among its settleable networks. All three paid routes now return \code{402}
responses a buyer could act on. The rail is live; what it sells is still
wrong.

\section{Market Context}
\label{sec:market}

\subsection{A note on sources}

Figures in this section come from the x402scan indexer's open tRPC endpoint,
corroborated independently by x402station, which agrees with it on
service-layer call counts to within two calls. Three widely-cited sources were
examined and rejected: one aggregator's last entry predates 2026 by months,
another's headline metrics are materially wrong, and---the trap worth
naming---the protocol's own homepage displays a \emph{frozen} counter. That
snapshot, roughly \$24M across 72M transactions, was captured in June 2026 and
has been reprinted since as though current, including by trade press. An
earlier draft of this paper repeated a similarly stale figure. Anyone
sizing this market should pull it from an indexer's API and check that the
number moves.

\subsection{Aggregate}

x402 volume peaked in November 2025 at \textbf{\$28.8M} across 68.6M
transactions and has fallen roughly \textbf{96\%} since
(Table~\ref{tab:volume}). August 2026 records \$1.18M across 14.9M
transactions.

The shape of that decline matters more than its depth. Transaction \emph{count}
has recovered to 12--15M/month, close to the December 2025 level, while the
average payment collapsed from \$0.42 to \$0.079. Activity returned; revenue
did not. A recovery visible only in transaction counts is farm-shaped, not
demand-shaped.

\subsection{How much of it is real}

Several structural signals indicate that a large share of this volume is not
commerce. The second-largest all-time buyer address is also the largest
all-time seller, at \$11.5M in each direction. A single counterparty pair
accounts for 26.5M transactions. Of 5{,}480 registered origins only 3{,}832
ever transacted, and 17 of the 40 highest all-time earners have recorded no
transaction since 2025. The 2025 peak also carried outright non-utility: a
pay-to-mint memecoin required 1 USDC per mint and processed over 150{,}000
transactions in its first month~\citep{chainalysis2026}.

The consequential figure for a prospective seller is therefore not total
volume but the \emph{discoverable service layer}: endpoints an agent could
find and buy from. That layer is 290{,}806 calls---about \textbf{1.7\%} of all
transactions---worth roughly \textbf{\$9{,}850 per month}. Everything above it
is inference routing, stablecoin conversion, and self-dealing. \$9{,}850 per
month, not \$1.18M, is the market a new listed seller is entering.

\subsection{Which turnstiles earn}

Earnings are severely concentrated. The top ten sellers hold \textbf{72.5\%}
of all volume. Against 22{,}556 sellers active in a 30-day window sharing
\$1.31M, the mean is about \$58/month and the median is certainly under a
dollar. Table~\ref{tab:sellers} names the leaders.

Every leading category sells \emph{live compute or live data}: something that
did not exist before the request and cannot be fetched from a static file. The
common factor is not subject matter but non-substitutability at the moment of
purchase. An archive is the opposite---static by definition, and cheapest to
serve by publishing once.

Prices cluster tightly. The median listed call is \$0.02, with modes at
\$0.001, \$0.005, \$0.01, and \$0.10; the effective clearing price is
\textbf{\$0.01} and the floor is \$0.001. The \$5.00 charged by this archive's
\code{/bulk} endpoint sat two to three orders of magnitude above the market
without a corresponding claim to uniqueness.

\subsection{The identity category is occupied, and it is not paying}

An earlier draft of this report stated that no x402 seller of identity,
namespaces, or agent messaging could be found, and treated the gap as an
unserved niche. That was wrong, and the correction matters because
\S\ref{sec:strategy} rests on it. Such sellers exist:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Palmyr} sells social handles at \$0.005 and domains at
\$10--\$100. Earnings: roughly \textbf{\$53} over ten weeks.
\item \textbf{Agent Exchange Lounge} sells admission to an agent social
network at \$0.05--\$0.09. Earnings: \textbf{\$4.22} from 30 payers.
\item \textbf{shizu.me} runs an agent mailbox. Earnings: \textbf{\$1.79} from
37 payers.
\end{itemize}

So the category has been built, priced, and tested by at least three operators,
and it earns in the tens of dollars. No comparable seller of ENS or Basename
registrations, XMTP messaging, or social-protocol writes was found, so the
upper end of the identity market remains genuinely untested---but the low end
is occupied and unremunerative. The honest reading is that these are small
unknown operators without audience, which bounds what the mechanism earns
\emph{absent distribution}; it does not establish what it earns \emph{with}
distribution. That distinction is the entire remaining case for the strategy
below, and it should be held loosely.

\section{Strategy}
\label{sec:strategy}

The audit reduces to two constraints that any candidate product must satisfy.
\textbf{Non-substitutability:} a rational agent must be unable to obtain the
good more cheaply elsewhere, including from the seller's own free tier.
\textbf{Discoverability:} per M4, a correct and well-priced endpoint that
nothing finds earns exactly as much as a broken one. Table~\ref{tab:strategy}
scores six options.

\begin{table}[t]
\centering
\footnotesize
\begin{tabularx}{\columnwidth}{@{}Xccc@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Strategy} & \textbf{Sub.} & \textbf{Disc.} & \textbf{Ready} \\
\midrule
A. Derived data (status quo) & fail & fail & yes \\
B. Metered rendering & pass & fail & part \\
C. Agent handles & pass & untested & part \\
D. Paid agent chatroom & pass & untested & part \\
E. Provenance receipts & pass & weak & yes \\
F. Exit x402 & --- & --- & --- \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}
\caption{Options against the two binding constraints. \emph{Sub.} asks whether
the buyer can get it free elsewhere; \emph{Disc.} whether the thing can
plausibly be found; \emph{Ready} whether the infrastructure exists today. C and
D score \emph{untested} rather than \emph{pass}: comparable services exist and
earn tens of dollars, but none has been run with an audience attached.}
\label{tab:strategy}
\end{table}

\textbf{A. Derived data} fails both constraints and is the status quo. It
should be withdrawn or repriced to zero, since a price nobody should rationally
pay is worse than no price.

\textbf{B. Metered rendering} would sell what the \ac{} runtime uniquely
does---evaluating KidLisp, rendering pieces to frames and tapes. It is
genuinely non-substitutable compute and sits in the highest-volume market
category. Its weakness is demand: no agent currently knows it wants a KidLisp
render, so the seller must create the demand it serves.

\textbf{C. Agent handles} sells a name in the \ac{} namespace. This is the
strongest fit, for a reason worth stating plainly: a namespace is
server-authoritative and therefore \emph{constitutively} non-substitutable. An
agent cannot compute \code{@name} for itself at any price, from any dataset,
with any amount of inference. Unlike rendering, the scarcity is structural
rather than economic. The infrastructure substantially exists---handle
registration, permanent-handle allocation, and chat all run in production
today---and the receipt-signing code repaired in \S\ref{sec:impl} generalizes
almost unchanged from license ownership to handle ownership.

\textbf{D. A paid agents-only chatroom} extends C, and inverts a problem into
a product: every agent-accessible commons has a flooding problem, and pricing
messages is simultaneously the moderation mechanism and the revenue. One
engineering caution: settlement is a network round trip with a 120-second
timeout in our own quoted terms, so per-message settlement would put a
blockchain confirmation in the conversational hot path. The workable shapes
are a one-time handle purchase with unmetered speech, a prepaid message
balance decremented locally, or admission priced per session.

Options C and D also address discovery in a way A and B cannot. A priced
dataset is not a story; a room where machines pay to talk to each other is
one, and per M4 the shortage was never buyers' willingness but their
attention. This is now the \emph{only} remaining argument for C and D, since
\S\ref{sec:market} shows the mechanism itself has been tried and earns in the
tens of dollars. The claim being made is narrow and falsifiable: that those
operators lacked an audience, and that the same mechanism attached to an
existing practice performs differently. If that is wrong, C and D earn what
Palmyr earns.

\textbf{E. Provenance receipts} generalizes the one component that already
works: selling signed, verifiable statements about artifacts. Real, but small.

\subsection{A concrete proposal}

Combining C and D while respecting the settlement-latency constraint yields a
design with one payment and no per-message blockchain confirmation:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{A handle is bought once.} An agent requests
\code{/api/agent/@name}, receives a \code{402} with the price, pays, and
receives a signed ownership receipt---the \S\ref{sec:impl} receipt structure
with \code{work} replaced by \code{handle}. Verification stays free and
stateless.
\item \textbf{The name is the admission.} Holding a handle grants speech in
the agents' room; the chat layer checks the signature, not a balance, so the
conversational path touches no chain.
\item \textbf{Scarcity is priced, not rationed.} Short and dictionary-word
handles cost more, exactly as every namespace in history has worked.
\end{enumerate}

Pricing should sit near demonstrated tolerance rather than this archive's
previous guesses: the \$5.00 \code{/bulk} price was set against no comparable
and, per M4, tested against no buyer. The observed clearing price is \$0.01,
and the one comparable handle seller charges \$0.005. Permanence justifies
some premium over a per-call price, but not the low-single-dollar figure an
earlier draft proposed---that would be two orders of magnitude above the only
handle price the market has actually seen. A defensible range is \$0.10 to
\$1.00, with the caveat that the seller charging \$0.005 earned \$53, so price
is evidently not the binding variable at this scale.

The honest failure mode is a room that agents visit once. The version with
durable value gives them something absent from the current landscape---a
neutral, persistent place to leave messages for other agents across
sessions---which is coordination infrastructure rather than novelty. The
cheapest decisive test costs nothing: register handles for agents this
practice already runs, let them talk for free, and observe whether the
transcript is worth reading. If it is not interesting unpriced, no price
improves it.

\textbf{F. Exit} deserves explicit statement, and \S\ref{sec:market}
strengthens it considerably. The addressable market is not the \$1.18M/month
headline but the \$9{,}850/month discoverable service layer, shared among tens
of thousands of sellers with a median under a dollar, in an economy down 96\%
from its peak and carrying visible wash traffic. A new niche seller should
expect a negligible share of a small number. Measured strictly as revenue,
every option above is outperformed by the practice's existing grant and client
lanes, and any decision to continue should be justified by positioning,
research value, or artistic interest rather than by projected income. This
report recommends continuing on those grounds, and recommends against
continuing on financial ones.

\section{Ethics, Privacy, and Limitations}

\textbf{Honest paywalls.} The license endpoint states in its own payload that
the assets it licenses are publicly reachable. A paywall that implies it gates
what it does not gate is a lie the buyer discovers in one request, and the
same standard condemns \S\ref{sec:evidence}'s M3: charging for derived free
data was not fraud, but it was not defensible either, and it is documented
here rather than quietly repriced.

\textbf{No purchase records.} Verification is stateless by construction, so
the archive holds no ledger of which agent licensed which work.

\textbf{Credential scope.} The settlement credential carries read-only
permissions---no trade, transfer, or withdrawal authority---and is pinned to
the production host's address. A consequence worth recording: rebuilding that
host on a new address silently breaks payment.

\textbf{Limitations.} M4's log window is hours, not months
(\S\ref{sec:evidence}). No end-to-end mainnet purchase has been executed: the
rail is verified to the point of settlement capability, not through a completed
sale, so M6 establishes capability rather than function until one buyer pays
once.

The market figures come from a single indexer's API, corroborated on the
service layer by one independent index but not otherwise cross-checked against
raw chain data; one prominent dashboard could not be reached at all. Given the
wash-trading signals in \S\ref{sec:market}, headline volume should be read as
an upper bound on real commerce rather than a measurement of it. Two drafts of
this paper carried wrong ecosystem figures---first a peak understated by more
than fivefold, then a stale counter---which is recorded here because the
failure is instructive rather than embarrassing: in this sector, reputable
secondary sources reprint frozen numbers, and a figure should not be trusted
until it is observed to change.

\section{Conclusion}

An archive was wired for machine payment, advertised properly, and could not
have earned a dollar---first because its facilitator settled only test
networks, then because its verification link asked for a proof it had not
been given, and finally because most of what it sold was already free next
door. Two of those were bugs. The third was a category error, and it is the
one that generalizes: the work of selling to machines is not building the
turnstile but finding something on the other side of it that the buyer cannot
simply compute.

Everything the archive knows can be recomputed from files it publishes. A name
cannot. That asymmetry, rather than any property of the payment protocol, is
where the next version of this experiment should begin---tempered by the
finding that three operators already sell names to agents and have earned
under \$60 between them. The asymmetry is real; that it converts into revenue
is a hypothesis, and the surrounding economy gives no encouragement about its
size. What the census does settle is that this was never a plumbing problem.
The turnstile now works perfectly, and the room behind it is still the
question.
